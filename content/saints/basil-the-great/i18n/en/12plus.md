### A family of saints

Our holy father Basil the Great was born around the year 330 in Caesarea, the chief city of Cappadocia, during the reign of the holy emperor Constantine. His family was a nest of saints. His grandmother Macrina had learned the faith from the disciples of St Gregory the Wonderworker; his father Basil was a famous teacher of rhetoric; his mother Emmelia, his sister Macrina and his brothers Gregory of Nyssa and Peter of Sebaste are all honored by the Church as saints.

From childhood Basil showed a quick mind and a love of learning. His father taught him first, and then he studied in Caesarea and in Constantinople with the best teachers of the age. At last he went to Athens, the most famous city of learning in the world, where he studied philosophy, rhetoric, astronomy, medicine and every science of the time.

### Two roads in Athens

In Athens Basil found a friend for life: Gregory of Nazianzus, later called the Theologian. The two young men were of one soul. They lived as if they had one body and one heart, and they avoided the noisy feasts and theaters of the city. Gregory later wrote that they knew only two roads in Athens: the one that led to the churches and their teachers there, and the one that led to their schools. They studied so diligently that they surpassed all their fellow students, and their teachers wondered at them. Among the students in Athens in those years was a prince named Julian, who would later become emperor and turn away from Christ.

Basil came to understand that the wisdom of this world, however beautiful, is empty without the knowledge of God. St Dimitri tells that he left Athens with his teacher Eubulus to travel through Egypt and Palestine, to learn from the holy monks who lived in the deserts. In Jerusalem, Basil and Eubulus asked Bishop Maximus to baptize them in the river Jordan. As Basil came up out of the water, a fiery light shone around him, and a dove flew from the river up toward heaven, so that all who stood there glorified God.

### Monk and priest

Returning to Cappadocia, Basil gave away his property and went to live as a monk in a wild and beautiful place in Pontus, beside the river Iris. Gregory joined him there. Together they prayed, fasted, worked with their hands and studied the Holy Scriptures. Many others came to live with them, and Basil wrote rules for monks that are followed in Orthodox monasteries to this day.

The bishop of Caesarea, Eusebius, called Basil back to the city and ordained him a priest. Those were hard times for the Church. The heresy of Arius, which denied that the Son of God is truly God, had spread everywhere, and the emperor Valens favored the heretics. Basil became the bishop's right hand, preaching every day and defending the true faith.

### The famine

About the year 368 a terrible drought struck Cappadocia. There was no rain, the crops failed, and a great famine followed. The rich stored up their grain and sold it at high prices, while the poor died of hunger in the streets. Basil preached to the rich with burning words: *"The bread you hoard belongs to the hungry. The coat you keep in your closet belongs to the naked. The money you bury belongs to the poor."* His preaching opened many storehouses.

He sold everything that remained of his own inheritance and bought food. Then he gathered the hungry together — Christians and pagans, even those who were not of his faith — and set up great kettles of soup and vegetables. Wearing an apron, he cooked and served them with his own hands, washed their feet and spoke to them of God.

[[scene:famine]]

Later, when he became bishop, he built outside the city a whole new town of mercy, which people called the *Basiliad*: a hospital, a home for the poor and for travelers, a shelter for lepers, with houses for doctors and workshops where the poor could learn a trade. It was one of the first great hospitals in the world.

### The gold of Caesarea

St Dimitri tells that when the emperor Julian, Basil's old fellow student, turned back to the pagan gods and marched against the Persians, he passed by Caesarea and threatened to destroy the city on his return. He demanded a great sum of gold. The people brought what they had — their gold coins, rings and jewelry — and gathered it in the church. Basil and his flock prayed and fasted for many days on a nearby mountain. In a vision Basil saw the Most Holy Theotokos sending the holy martyr Mercurius against the persecutor, and soon the news came that Julian had been killed in Persia.

Basil then returned the gold to the people. A beloved later tradition tells that, since no one could tell whose coins were whose, he had them baked into small loaves and handed out, and each family found its own treasure in its bread. That is why, on St Basil's day, many Orthodox families cut the *Vasilopita*, St Basil's bread, with a coin hidden inside.

### "Perhaps you have never met a bishop"

In the year 370 Basil was chosen archbishop of Caesarea. The emperor Valens, who wanted to force all the bishops to accept the Arian teaching, came to Cappadocia. He sent ahead his prefect Modestus, a hard and proud man, to frighten Basil into obedience.

Modestus summoned Basil and threatened him with confiscation of his goods, with exile, with torture and with death. Basil answered calmly: "Is that all? None of this frightens me. Confiscation cannot touch a man who owns nothing but these worn clothes and a few books. I know no exile, for the whole earth is the Lord's, and wherever I am sent I will be His guest. Torture cannot hurt a body as weak as mine; the first blow would end it. And death would be a kindness, for it would bring me sooner to God, for whom I live."

[[scene:modestus]]

Modestus was astonished. "No one has ever spoken to me with such boldness," he said. "Perhaps," Basil replied, "you have never met a bishop before." The prefect went to the emperor and said: "We are defeated by the leader of this Church. He is above threats."

Valens decided to send Basil into exile. But when he took up the pen to sign the order, it broke in his hand; he took another, and it broke too; and a third time the same thing happened. His hand trembled, and he tore up the paper. Soon afterwards the emperor's little son fell gravely ill, and Valens, humbled, asked Basil to pray for him. Basil came, and the child grew better.

### The Divine Liturgy of St Basil

Basil was a tireless shepherd, writer and teacher. He wrote books defending the faith in the Holy Trinity and in the divinity of the Holy Spirit; he explained the six days of creation in sermons that are still read with wonder; and he composed the prayers of the Divine Liturgy that bears his name, which the Orthodox Church celebrates on his feast, on the Sundays of Great Lent and on the great feasts of Nativity and Theophany.

Worn out by fasting, labor and illness, he fell asleep in the Lord on the first of January in the year 379, at the age of only forty-nine. The whole city mourned him — not only Christians, but pagans and Jews as well — for he had been a father to all.

### A light for the Church

Together with St Gregory the Theologian and St John Chrysostom he is honored as one of the Three Holy Hierarchs, the great teachers of the Church. St Basil teaches us to love learning but to love God more, to share our bread with the hungry, and to fear nothing but sin. Holy father Basil, pray to Christ God for us!
